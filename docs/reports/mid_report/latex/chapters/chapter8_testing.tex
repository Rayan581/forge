\chapter{Testing and Early Validation}
\label{chap:testing}

\section{Testing Strategy}
To guarantee system stability, algorithmic reliability, and gameplay solvability, Project FORGE employs a structured multi-tier verification strategy. Because FORGE operates at the intersection of game engine physics, procedural generation, and machine learning, testing spans four distinct levels:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Unit Testing:} Validating core mathematical routines, observation vector normalization, action denormalization clamping, the non-uniform $CR_0$ weighting and the $s_0$ seed (\texttt{pytest} and headless GDScript tests).
    \item \textbf{Engine Integration Testing:} Verifying signal dispatching between \texttt{Player.gd}, \texttt{Enemy.gd}, \texttt{Main.gd} and \texttt{TelemetryLogger.gd} inside the real game scene (headless TC-01), plus manual play-testing of collisions and projectiles.
    \item \textbf{Automated Headless CI Solvability Testing:} Executing Godot 4.7.2 in \texttt{--headless} mode via GitHub Actions on 500 seeds for each room count from 3 to 9 (3,500 layouts), checking matching door pairs and breadth-first-search reachability of every room. Because layouts are spanning trees by construction, this is a regression test of that property rather than an independent proof.
    \item \textbf{Simulated Policy Evaluation:} Evaluating the offline PPO policy in Gymnasium (training curves over three seeds and 100 paired 30-level episodes per simulated player profile, Section~\ref{sec:policy_eval}) to verify pacing and detect reward hacking.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Test Results and Analysis}
All 10 formal test cases pass on the submitted code. TC-01 and TC-02 failed when first automated and pass after fixes DEF-05 and DEF-06 (Section~\ref{sec:defects}). Key findings include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neural Forward Pass Performance:} Measured with the reproducible script \texttt{tests/benchmark\_inference.gd} (headless Godot 4.7.2, 200 warm-up and 1,000 timed calls of \texttt{PPOPolicy.predict()} on the shipped weights), the mean latency was $0.212$\,ms (median $0.210$\,ms, 95th percentile $0.223$\,ms, 99th percentile $0.241$\,ms, maximum $0.355$\,ms), with no invalid outputs. The mean uses about $1.3\%$ of the $16.6$\,ms frame budget and the maximum roughly $2.1\%$, inside the $<1.0$\,ms bound of NFR-06. These figures were measured on the development PC before fix DEF-07, which only replaces seven output $\tanh$ calls with clamps. Re-running the fixed code on a shared cloud VM (headless Godot 4.7.2) gave median $0.357$\,ms, p95 $0.461$\,ms and p99 $0.608$\,ms, with a single $2.2$\,ms outlier attributable to VM scheduling. The benchmark therefore gates on the median, and the forward pass runs only once per floor transition.
    \item \textbf{Topological Solvability:} Over 500 seeds for each room count from 3 to 9, all 3,500 layouts had every room reachable from the start room through matching door pairs. This confirms the construction is implemented correctly; it does not test traversability inside rooms.
    \item \textbf{Cold-Start Numerical Stability:} The non-uniform weighting formula (Equations~\ref{eq:weights}--\ref{eq:cr0_formula}, with the clamp to $[10, 95]$) was evaluated on three scripted profiles by the headless test \texttt{tests/test\_cold\_start.gd}, which feeds the per-floor records through \texttt{TelemetryLogger} and \texttt{DifficultyManager}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Novice (pace 10, 12, 15, 15, 15\,s/room; deaths 0, 0, 2, 2, 2; damage 60, 80, 120, 120, 120): $CR_0 = 19.65$; $s_0[0..2] = (0.94, 0.50, 0.93)$.
        \item Intermediate ($9$\,s/room, one death on Floors 4 and 5, $70$ damage per floor): $CR_0 = 60.25$; $s_0[0..2] = (0.60, 0.18, 0.70)$.
        \item Expert ($4.5$\,s/room, no deaths, no damage): raw score $100$, clamped to $CR_0 = 95$; $s_0[0..2] = (0.30, 0.00, 0.00)$.
    \end{itemize}
    With these seeds the shipped policy sets Floor 6 to enemy health $20$ / $20$ / $120$ and hazard density $0.20$ / $0.60$ / $0.80$ for the three profiles. The Novice and Intermediate profiles thus receive the same (minimum) enemy health and differ only in hazard density, another sign of the limited personalization discussed in Section~\ref{sec:policy_params}.
\end{itemize}


\section{Training Convergence and Unseen-Profile Evaluation}
\label{sec:policy_eval}
To address the committee's request for evidence that the learned policy is better than an untrained one, the PPO training run was repeated with logging enabled (same environment, network ($8\rightarrow64\rightarrow64\rightarrow7$), hyper-parameters and 300{,}000 timesteps as \texttt{train\_ppo\_difficulty.py}) for three random seeds. The shipped \texttt{policy\_weights.json} (identical, weight for weight, to the saved SB3 checkpoint \texttt{ppo\_difficulty\_model.zip}) was evaluated separately using a NumPy re-implementation of the forward pass in \texttt{PPOPolicy.gd}, after fix DEF-07 (clipped rather than $\tanh$-squashed output).

\subsection{Training Curves}
Figure~\ref{fig:training_curves} shows the three runs. The mean episode return (30 levels per episode) rises from about $-6.5$ at initialization to $16.0$, $15.8$ and $16.2$ for seeds 0, 1 and 2 respectively, and flattens after roughly 250k timesteps. The policy's exploration standard deviation decreases steadily from $1.0$ to about $0.6$ (with small upticks in under $12\%$ of updates), and the critic loss settles near a stable plateau; no run diverged.

\subsection{Trained vs.\ Randomly Initialized Policy on Simulated Player Profiles}
\textbf{Protocol.} The simulated player in training starts with skill $\sim U(0.3,0.6)$ and improves by $U(0, 0.01)$ per level, so over a 30-level episode skill reaches about $0.60$ on average (95th percentile $0.74$, maximum $0.81$). Evaluation used profiles with initial skill $0.10$, $0.20$ (novice) and $0.90$ (expert), which lie outside the training distribution; $0.80$, at its extreme edge; $0.70$, in its upper tail (3\% of training steps); and $0.45$ as an in-range reference. Each profile was played for 100 episodes of 30 levels using identical random seeds for every policy (paired comparison). The baseline is a randomly initialized policy: ten untrained networks with the same architecture, deterministic output, averaged. Because untrained outputs are close to zero, this baseline effectively holds every parameter near the middle of its range, i.e.\ it behaves like a fixed mid-difficulty setting. It is therefore a weak baseline; comparisons with the heuristic fallback and the static $+8\%$ mode are scheduled. Besides the training reward, two reward-independent metrics are reported: the pacing error $|t_{\text{room}}-7.5\,\text{s}|/7.5\,\text{s}$ and deaths per level.

\textbf{Results.} The shipped policy achieves a higher mean reward than the random-initialized policy on all six profiles (paired $t$-test, $p<10^{-27}$ in every case; paired effect size $d_z$ from $1.6$ at skill $0.45$ to $14.6$ at skill $0.10$). With 100 simulated episodes even small differences are significant, so the effect sizes and the reward-independent metrics matter more than the $p$-values. The three re-trained runs show the same ordering. The largest gains are for novices, where the untrained policy keeps the game far too hard (2.3--2.7 deaths per level) while the trained policy lowers difficulty and reduces the pacing error from $0.59$ to $0.11$ ($0.10$ skill) and from $0.41$ to $0.09$ ($0.20$ skill).

\textbf{Limitations.} (i) All profiles are simulated; the comparison does not replace the human study planned for FYP-2. (ii) On the in-range profile ($0.45$) the untrained policy is already close to the pacing target, because its near-zero actions map to mid-range difficulty parameters; the trained policy still earns a higher reward through fewer deaths, but its pacing error is larger ($0.24$ vs.\ $0.10$). (iii) For skilled and expert profiles the trained policy is better than the baseline but remains well away from the pacing target ($0.33$--$0.59$ error), because the bounded difficulty ranges saturate; re-trained seeds also vary more there (one seed is no better than the baseline at skill $0.70$). The reward is the quantity PPO was optimized for, which is why pacing error and deaths are reported as secondary checks.

\subsection{What the Shipped Policy Actually Adjusts}
\label{sec:policy_params}
Table~\ref{tab:policy_params} shows the mean parameter values the shipped policy chose over the same 100 episodes per profile. Personalization happens through enemy health, hazard density, resource scarcity and, for strong players, room count. Enemy damage stays at its minimum and enemy speed at its maximum for every profile. This is a reward-shaping artifact of the simulator rather than a design choice: low damage reduces simulated deaths, and the simulator's time model rewards fast enemies. Enemy aggro ($a_3$) and resource scarcity ($a_5$) do not affect the simulated player or the reward at all, so their outputs are effectively untrained. The learned action log-std confirms this: it stays near $0$ for those two dimensions ($\sigma \approx 0.9$) but falls to $\sigma \approx 0.39$--$0.49$ for the other five. Correcting the simulator and reward, and retraining, is the first task of Iteration 3.

\section{Edge and Failure Case Analysis}
We deliberately subjected the architecture to severe edge and failure scenarios:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Scenario 1: Missing or Malformed Policy Weights.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Condition:} \texttt{policy\_weights.json} was deleted from \texttt{res://ai/}.
        \item \textit{Observed Behavior:} \texttt{PPOPolicy.gd} issued a warning to the console, set \texttt{loaded = false}, and engaged \texttt{\_fallback\_heuristic()}. The game continued without crashing, adjusting parameters from clearing time, deaths and damage taken. (A file that parses as JSON but has wrong layer shapes is not yet handled; a shape check is planned.)
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scenario 2: Catastrophic Player Skill Shock (offline probe).}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Condition:} Floor 3 with the Intermediate preset active (enemy health 60, damage 20); the observation encodes three deaths and an $80$\,s clear ($s_{\text{deaths}} = 1.0$, $s_{\text{time}} = 0.89$, damage ratio $1.0$). The shipped weights were evaluated with the NumPy mirror of \texttt{PPOPolicy.gd}.
        \item \textit{Observed Behavior:} The policy eases health, damage and hazards in one large step: enemy health $60 \rightarrow 20$ ($-67\%$), damage $20 \rightarrow 5$ ($-75\%$), hazard density $0.35 \rightarrow 0.05$. At the same time it \emph{raises} enemy speed from $125$ to $220$\,px/s, its maximum. Parameters stay inside their bounds, so the game remains playable, but the step is neither gradual nor uniformly easier; this motivates the inter-floor rate limiter (Chapter~\ref{chap:mgmt}) and the retraining in Section~\ref{sec:policy_params}.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scenario 3: Zero-Damage Speedrunning (offline probe).}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Condition:} Same starting state; the observation encodes a $10$\,s clear with no deaths and no damage taken ($s_{\text{time}} = 0.11$).
        \item \textit{Observed Behavior:} The policy escalates enemy health ($60 \rightarrow 120$, $+100\%$), hazard density ($0.35 \rightarrow 0.80$) and speed ($125 \rightarrow 209$\,px/s) but lowers enemy damage ($20 \rightarrow 5$). The response is therefore only partly consistent with ``escalate for a skilled player'' and illustrates that the policy was trained on a simulated player, which is why real-player validation is planned in FYP-2.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Current Validation Status}
All known defects (DEF-01 to DEF-09) are fixed, and the automated suites pass: 3,500/3,500 reachability layouts, TC-01, TC-02 and TC-10 in headless Godot 4.7.2, and the \texttt{pytest} suite. Open items carried into Iteration 3 are the partial personalization of the trained policy (Section~\ref{sec:policy_params}), frame-time measurement (NFR-05), and the comparison against non-adaptive baselines.
